\section{Powertrain Modeling and Motor Selection}

\begin{multicols}{2}
The foundation of the 2026 Formula~1 hybrid powertrain begins with accurately quantifying the opposing environmental forces to derive the requisite mechanical output of the Motor Generator Unit--Kinetic (MGU-K). This section details the fundamental longitudinal vehicle dynamics, motor rating selection, and the implementation of advanced control algorithms required to manage the tractive power effectively. It addresses the assignment mandate: \textit{``You have to design an EV. First step is to decide motor rating''} --- adapted here to the F1 MGU-K context.

% -----------------------------------------------------------
% 7.1  Longitudinal Vehicle Dynamics  (assignment item 1)
% -----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Longitudinal Vehicle Dynamics}

To define the continuous and peak operational limits of the MGU-K, the total tractive effort ($F_{te}$) required at the tire contact patch must be explicitly calculated. This effort must overcome four distinct environmental and inertial components: Aerodynamic Drag ($F_{ad}$), Rolling Resistance ($F_{rr}$), Gradient Force ($F_{g}$), and Acceleration Force ($F_{a}$).

The 2026 F1 vehicle parameters are established as follows: minimum mass ($m$) of \SI{800}{kg} \cite{FIA_2026_Chassis_Regulations}, frontal area ($A$) of \SI{1.5}{m^2} (derived from the 2026 regulated width and ride height), and a target top speed ($v$) of \SI{348}{km/h} (\SI{96.7}{m/s}), matching the simulated peak velocity at Monza. The air density ($\rho$) is \SI{1.225}{kg/m^3}, and the rolling resistance coefficient ($\mu_r$) is 0.015 for Pirelli slick compounds at operating temperature.

\textbf{1. Aerodynamic Drag ($F_{ad}$):} As velocity increases, the required force to displace the fluid volume scales quadratically. At the Monza peak speed of \SI{348}{km/h} (\SI{96.7}{m/s}), the vehicle operates in X-mode (ultra-low-drag configuration) with a drag coefficient ($C_d$) of 0.65. However, for the worst-case motor sizing derivation, the Z-mode (high-downforce) coefficient of $C_d = 1.00$ is conservatively applied:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:aero_drag}
\begin{split}
F_{ad} &= \frac{1}{2} \rho v^2 C_d A \\
&= 0.5 \cdot 1.225 \cdot (96.7)^2 \cdot 1.00 \cdot 1.5 \\
&= 0.5 \cdot 1.225 \cdot 9350.9 \cdot 1.5 \\
&\approx 8599.6\,\mathrm{N}
\end{split}
\end{equation}

\textbf{2. Rolling Resistance ($F_{rr}$):} The hysteresis losses generated by the deformation of the viscoelastic Pirelli tire compound against the Monza asphalt are quantified by the coefficient of rolling resistance ($\mu_r \approx 0.015$). With gravitational acceleration ($g$) at \SI{9.81}{m/s^2}:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:rolling_res}
\begin{split}
F_{rr} &= m \cdot g \cdot \mu_r \\
&= 800 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.015 \\
&= 117.7\,\mathrm{N}
\end{split}
\end{equation}

\textbf{3. Gradient/Hill Climbing Force ($F_{g}$):} The Autodromo Nazionale Monza is a predominantly flat circuit. However, to ensure operational viability on banked sections and camber transitions, a nominal design gradient ($\alpha$) of $1^\circ$ ($\sim$1.7\,\% grade) is conservatively assumed:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:gradient_force}
\begin{split}
F_{g} &= m \cdot g \cdot \sin(\alpha) \\
&= 800 \cdot 9.81 \cdot \sin(1^\circ) \\
&= 800 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.01745 \\
&= 137.0\,\mathrm{N}
\end{split}
\end{equation}

\textbf{4. Acceleration Force ($F_{a}$):} To achieve a target 0--\SI{100}{km/h} sprint in approximately 2.0 seconds (characteristic of a 2026 F1 car deploying \SI{350}{kW} electrical torque), an average linear acceleration ($a$) of approximately \SI{13.9}{m/s^2} (\SI{1.4}{g}) is required:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:accel_force}
\begin{split}
F_{a} &= m \cdot a \\
&= 800 \cdot 13.9 \\
&= 11{,}120.0\,\mathrm{N}
\end{split}
\end{equation}

The cumulative tractive effort ($F_{te\_peak}$) under maximum acceleration at the Z-mode drag penalty is the direct summation of these vectors:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:total_tractive}
\begin{split}
F_{te\_peak} &= F_{ad} + F_{rr} + F_{g} + F_{a} \\
&= 8599.6 + 117.7 + 137.0 + 11{,}120.0 \\
&= 19{,}974.3\,\mathrm{N}
\end{split}
\end{equation}
\end{multicols}

\subsection{Motor Rating Determination}

Translating the linearly derived tractive effort into rotational MGU-K requirements demands accounting for the mechanical drivetrain efficiency ($\eta \approx 0.95$ for the F1-spec gearbox and differential) and the dynamic tire radius ($r = \SI{0.33}{m}$ for Pirelli 18-inch slicks).

The absolute peak power ($P_{peak\_motor}$) required at the motor shaft during maximum velocity acceleration is:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:motor_peak_pwr}
\begin{split}
P_{peak\_motor} &= \frac{F_{te\_peak} \cdot v}{\eta} \\
&= \frac{19{,}974.3 \cdot 96.7}{0.95} \\
&\approx 2{,}034{,}100\,\mathrm{W} \approx 2034\,\mathrm{kW}
\end{split}
\end{equation}

This \SI{2034}{kW} figure represents the \emph{total} system power demand at the wheels under worst-case combined acceleration and drag. The ICE contributes approximately \SI{450}{kW} mechanically, and the remaining \SI{350}{kW} electrical MGU-K deployment provides instantaneous torque fill during the initial acceleration phase before the turbocharger spools. Therefore, the MGU-K is rated at the FIA-mandated \textbf{\SI{350}{kW} peak}.

Conversely, the nominal continuous power rating is defined by the steady-state energy harvesting during braking phases. At \SI{350}{kW} regeneration over \SI{12.5}{s} per lap (5 braking zones $\times$ \SI{2.5}{s}), the average continuous harvesting power is:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:motor_cont_pwr}
\begin{split}
P_{cont\_motor} &= \frac{P_{regen} \cdot t_{harvest}}{t_{lap}} \\
&= \frac{350{,}000 \cdot 12.5}{70} \\
&\approx 62{,}500\,\mathrm{W} \approx 62.5\,\mathrm{kW}
\end{split}
\end{equation}

Therefore, the finalized MGU-K specification dictates a continuous harvesting rating of \SI{62.5}{kW} with a peak transient deployment capability of \SI{350}{kW}.

\subsection{Motor Selection: IPM vs. SPM}

The selection between a Surface Permanent Magnet (SPM) and Interior Permanent Magnet (IPM) motor dramatically influences the torque density and high-speed field-weakening capabilities of the \SI{648}{V} MGU-K architecture. This subsection addresses the assignment requirement to \textit{``mention if you are selecting SPM or IPM motor and explain all the torques in the motor and its control technique''}.

While an SPM motor simplifies the rotor construction by adhering the neodymium magnets to the outer rotor circumference, it suffers from a near-homogenous magnetic reluctance path ($L_d \approx L_q$), restricting it exclusively to magnetic alignment torque. Furthermore, the fragile mechanical retention of surface-mounted magnets limits rotational speeds, reducing power density --- an unacceptable constraint for an MGU-K operating at up to \SI{50{,}000}{RPM}.

Therefore, an \textbf{Interior Permanent Magnet (IPM)} topology is explicitly selected for the MGU-K powertrain. Embedding the magnets within the rotor lamination steel creates magnetic saliency ($L_q > L_d$). This asymmetric reluctance path unlocks a secondary, profound torque-producing component. The total electromagnetic torque ($T_e$) of the selected IPM motor is defined as:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:ipm_torque}
T_e = \underbrace{\frac{3}{2} p \lambda_f i_q}_{\text{Magnetic Torque}} + \underbrace{\frac{3}{2} p (L_d - L_q) i_d i_q}_{\text{Reluctance Torque}}
\end{equation}

where $p$ represents pole pairs, $\lambda_f$ is the permanent magnet flux linkage, and $i_d, i_q$ are the direct and quadrature axis stator currents. The first term, \textbf{Magnetic Torque}, is produced by the interaction between the stator MMF and the rotor permanent magnet field, proportional to $i_q$. The second term, \textbf{Reluctance Torque}, arises from the rotor's tendency to align its minimum-reluctance axis with the stator flux vector --- governed by the inductance inequality $(L_d - L_q) < 0$ for an IPM machine.

At lower base speeds (e.g., corner exit torque-fill), the controller biases current heavily towards $i_q$ to maximize magnetic alignment torque. As velocity increases towards the \SI{50{,}000}{RPM} regime, the controller manipulates $i_d$ (setting it negative), strategically leveraging the $(L_d - L_q)$ reluctance inequality to generate supplementary torque, enabling superior overall system efficiency and high-speed field-weakening performance compared directly against an SPM equivalent. The \SI{648}{V} DC bus from the 180S2P battery pack provides the necessary voltage headroom for these high-speed current vector transitions.

\subsection{Matching Control Method (FOC)}

Unlocking the complex reluctance torque of the IPM architecture necessitates an advanced Field Oriented Control (FOC) strategy, fundamentally superior to primitive Six-Step BLDC commutation or scalar V/Hz methods.

The FOC algorithm mathematically decouples the 3-phase AC stator currents ($i_a, i_b, i_c$) via consecutive Clarke and Park transformations into a synchronous rotating reference frame aligned with the internal rotor magnet flux. This reduces the complex AC equations into two controllable DC orthogonal components: flux-producing current ($i_d$) and torque-producing current ($i_q$).

\textbf{Maximum Torque Per Ampere (MTPA) Strategy:}
Unlike an SPM motor where MTPA strictly enforces $i_d = 0$, optimal control of the selected IPM motor requires a dynamic $i_d$ reference to maximize the combined magnetic and reluctance torque output per unit of stator current. The MTPA algorithm calculates the optimal negative $i_d$ trajectory as a function of the operational torque demand:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:ipm_mtpa}
i_d^* = \frac{\lambda_f}{2(L_q - L_d)} - \sqrt{ \frac{\lambda_f^2}{4(L_q - L_d)^2} + (i_q^*)^2 }
\end{equation}

For the \SI{350}{kW} MGU-K at \SI{648}{V}, this MTPA trajectory is critical during the torque-fill phase exiting slow corners (e.g., Monza's Variante del Rettifilo and Variante della Roggia). The MGU-K must inject maximum electromagnetic torque into the crankshaft instantaneously while the turbocharger organically spools up. The negative $i_d$ commanded by MTPA simultaneously produces reluctance torque \emph{and} performs field-weakening, extending the speed range beyond the base speed without exceeding the inverter's \SI{648}{V} DC bus limit.

Translation of the resultant Proportional-Integral (PI) controller reference voltages back into physical gating commands is achieved via Space Vector PWM (SVPWM). SVPWM synthesizes the rotating voltage vector by symmetrically modulating the eight fundamental switching states of the 3-phase Silicon Carbide (SiC) inverter bridge. This topology provides a 15\,\% superior utilization of the \SI{648}{V} DC bus compared to standard sinusoidal PWM, critical for extracting the full \SI{350}{kW} peak rating from the 180S2P Energy Store. The SVPWM sector identification and dwell-time calculations operate at a switching frequency of \SI{20}{kHz}, well above the electrical fundamental frequency of the MGU-K at maximum RPM, ensuring smooth torque delivery across the entire speed envelope.
